\begin{abstract}

A quantum circuit for first-quantization-based Hamiltonian simulation, capable
of operating on a small-scale quantum computer emulator with fewer than 30
qubits, was constructed and demonstrated for one- and two-dimensional
single-electron hydrogen atom models. (1) For small model sizes, Uniformly Controlled Rotation
(UCR) circuits were found to be a preferable choice for calculating the Coulomb
potential compared with conventional arithmetic gates in terms of qubit count
and circuit depth. (2) As a guideline for obtaining optimal accuracy within the
given resources, we established a method for handling the singularity (pole) of
Coulomb potential and optimizing the discretization parameters. Regarding the
singularity of Coulomb potential, we demonstrated that divergence could be
avoided and errors minimized by determining an effective value of the potential
function from a simple geometric average of the potential within a minute
circular region centered on the pole. (3) We proposed a method to determine the
optimal spatial grid spacing for a given number of qubits for coordinates, by
evaluating the tradeoff between the spatial discretization error caused by grid
coarseness and the domain truncation error caused by a finite domain range. For
the time evolution step size, rather than relying on the formal Trotter error
bound that assumes the worst-case scenario, we observed that sampling-theorem
based bounds focusing on the maximum frequency component of the wave function
are sufficient to achieve optimal accuracy while avoiding an unnecessary
increase in the quantum circuit depth. While the circuit implementation
demonstrated here is specific to the small-scale regime, the singularity
treatment and the parameter-selection procedure address the grid
discretization itself and therefore apply to grid-based first-quantization
simulations generally.

\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
In this study, we presented a circuit configuration capable of simulating the
full time evolution of a two-dimensional hydrogen atom model on a Qiskit
emulator. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to implement
grid-based time evolution calculations, including the Coulomb potential,
entirely as a quantum circuit and to confirm its operation on an emulator.

Regarding the handling of the Coulomb potential singularity, we proposed a
method using $\reff=\deltar/4$ as the effective distance to determine the
effective potential at the grid point containing the singularity in the 2D
model. Numerical experiments confirmed that this value minimizes the
energy error in the time-evolution simulation, and that it coincides with
the value derived from the geometric area average around the singularity,
clarifying the physical validity of this approach in a discretized space.

Furthermore, we established a procedure to determine the simulation parameters
that minimize the error for a given number of qubits ($\nb$). We showed that the
optimal spatial grid spacing, $\deltar$, can be determined from the trade-off
between the spatial discretization error and the domain truncation error.
Importantly, this spacing dictates the treatable range of
energy levels---from the ground state to higher-order excited states---for which
wave functions can be represented, thereby defining the effective dynamic range
of the simulation.

Moreover, regarding the time step, $\deltat$, of the Trotter decomposition, we
numerically confirmed that it can be determined not from the formal upper bound
of the Trotter error assuming the worst case, but from the requirement of the
sampling theorem (half-period condition) based on the maximum frequency
component of the wave function. Particularly when targeting states near the
ground state, we demonstrated that within the range satisfying this condition,
the spatial error becomes dominant over the temporal error (i.e., the error
reaches a lower bound), providing a guideline to avoid unnecessary increases in
quantum circuit depth.

\subsection{Limitations and Outlook}

The scope of the present study was intentionally restricted to a
single-electron hydrogen atom in one and two spatial dimensions, in order
to validate the singularity-handling and parameter-optimization
methodologies proposed here without the confounding effects of
electron-electron interaction and correlation that arise in multi-electron
systems. A full multi-electron circuit construction, including
antisymmetrized (Slater-determinant-based) initial-state preparation, has
already been demonstrated in our previous work
\cite{takahashi2024.doi:10.1021/acs.jctc.4c00708}; the outstanding
challenge is therefore not the construction of such circuits per se, but
reducing their qubit and gate-count requirements to a range that is
tractable on near-term emulators and hardware. Extending the methodology
established here to that qubit-efficient multi-electron setting is an
important direction for future work.

Similarly, for the 1D model we restricted our evaluation to the
odd-parity eigenstates. As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:simulator-model},
the $n=0$ ground state is not amenable to a finite-grid representation at
all---its wave function is the singular limit $|\psi|^2=\delta(x)$ with
divergent binding energy---while the $n>0$ even-parity states, although
well-defined with finite Balmer energies, possess a cusp (a discontinuous
slope) at the origin that is intrinsically harder to resolve accurately on
a finite grid than the smooth odd-parity states
\cite{Loudon1959.10.1119/1.1934950}. Extending the present
singularity-treatment and parameter-selection methodology to these
even-parity states, including the singular ground state, is left for
future work.

Regarding circuit implementation, the UCRZ-based approach is best
understood as a design well suited to pedagogical, small-scale systems:
within this study it clearly outperformed arithmetic circuits in qubit
count for the 1D and 2D models, but that advantage is lost once the
dimensionality increases to 3D
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:circuit-size-evaluation-results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:hamiltonian-gatecount}),
because the number of controlled rotations scales exponentially with the
size of the coordinate register. This is not primarily a matter that
further optimization of the UCRZ decomposition itself can be expected to
resolve; scaling beyond the pedagogical regime studied here is
understood, consistent with prior large-scale first-quantization
proposals, to require a qualitatively different toolkit---e.g. a QROM-based
potential evaluation combined with a Newton-Raphson-type iterative
inversion of the Coulomb kernel, together with time evolution via quantum
signal processing (QSP) rather than Trotterization---at the cost of a
substantially larger qubit count, in exchange for asymptotically lower
gate (T-)cost and circuit depth than the arithmetic- or UCRZ-based
Trotter approach considered here
\cite{YuanSu.PRXQuantum.2.040332, eklund2026.arXiv2603.19007}. The present
work should therefore be read as establishing an accurate, resource-light
baseline and parameter-selection methodology for this pedagogical regime,
rather than as a stepping stone toward scaling the UCRZ circuit itself.

It should be emphasized, however, that this scaling limitation is specific
to the circuit implementation, and not to the two methodological
contributions of this work. The treatment of the Coulomb singularity via
a local average potential and the procedure for selecting the spatial and
temporal discretization parameters both address properties of the
grid-based discretization of the Hamiltonian itself, independently of how
the potential is subsequently evaluated on a circuit. They are therefore
equally applicable to the large-scale, QROM- and QSP-based
first-quantization frameworks discussed above, in which the same
singularity must be regularized and the same grid spacing and time step
must be chosen. In this sense, these findings are expected to provide
practical guidelines for designing and executing grid-based quantum
simulations across scales---from the resource-constrained regime
demonstrated here to fully fault-tolerant implementations.
