\begin{abstract}

A quantum circuit for first-quantization-based Hamiltonian simulation, capable
of running on a small-scale quantum computer emulator with fewer than 30 qubits,
was constructed and demonstrated for one- and two-dimensional hydrogen-atom
models, the simplest settings sufficient to check the proposed circuits without
the confounding effects of electron-electron interaction and correlation. (1)
For simplified small models such as the 1D/2D hydrogen atom, Uniformly
Controlled Rotation Z (UCRZ) circuits were found valuable for constructing a
Coulomb potential circuit compact enough to fit on a small-scale emulator.
Although this approach does not scale to larger models, we compare it against a
conventional arithmetic-circuit implementation to identify the regime in which
UCRZ is preferable. (2) For the Coulomb singularity, we propose regularizing it
via a geometric average of the potential over a small circular region around the
pole, avoiding divergence and minimizing the resulting energy error. (3) To
select parameters minimizing error for a given number of qubits, we determine
the optimal spatial grid spacing from the tradeoff between spatial
discretization error and domain truncation error, and determine the time
evolution step size not from the formal worst-case Trotter error bound but from
a sampling-theorem-based bound on the wave function's maximum frequency
component, avoiding unnecessary circuit depth without sacrificing accuracy.
While the circuit implementation (1) is specific to the small-scale regime, the
singularity treatment (2) and parameter-selection procedure (3) address the grid
discretization itself and apply broadly to grid-based first-quantization
simulations.

\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
In this study, we presented a circuit configuration capable of simulating the
full time evolution of a two-dimensional hydrogen atom model on a Qiskit
emulator. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to implement
grid-based time evolution calculations, including the Coulomb potential,
entirely as a quantum circuit and to confirm its operation on an emulator.

Regarding the handling of the Coulomb potential singularity, we proposed a
method using $\reff=\deltar/4$ as the effective distance to determine the
effective potential at the grid point containing the singularity in the 2D
model. Numerical experiments confirmed that this value minimizes the
energy error in the time-evolution simulation, and that it coincides with
the value derived from the geometric area average around the singularity,
clarifying the physical validity of this approach in a discretized space.

Furthermore, we established a procedure to determine the simulation parameters
that minimize the error for a given number of qubits ($\nb$). We showed that the
optimal spatial grid spacing, $\deltar$, can be determined from the trade-off
between the spatial discretization error and the domain truncation error.
Importantly, this spacing dictates the treatable range of
energy levels---from the ground state to higher-order excited states---for which
wave functions can be represented, thereby defining the effective dynamic range
of the simulation.

Moreover, regarding the time step, $\deltat$, of the Trotter decomposition, we
numerically confirmed that it can be determined not from the formal upper bound
of the Trotter error assuming the worst case, but from the requirement of the
sampling theorem (half-period condition) based on the maximum frequency
component of the wave function. Particularly when targeting states near the
ground state, we demonstrated that within the range satisfying this condition,
the spatial error becomes dominant over the temporal error (i.e., the error
reaches a lower bound), providing a guideline to avoid unnecessary increases in
quantum circuit depth.

\subsection{Limitations and Outlook}

The scope of the present study was restricted to a single-electron
hydrogen atom in one and two spatial dimensions. This restriction was
primarily practical: a full multielectron circuit construction,
including antisymmetrized (Slater-determinant-based) initial-state
preparation, was already demonstrated in our previous work
\cite{takahashi2024.doi:10.1021/acs.jctc.4c00708}, but even at modest
system sizes its qubit and gate-count requirements exceed what is
tractable on the near-term emulators and hardware used here. Confining
the analysis to the single-electron case had the additional benefit of
validating the singularity-handling and parameter-optimization
methodologies proposed here without the confounding effects of
electron-electron interaction and correlation. Extending the methodology
established here to a qubit-efficient multielectron setting is an
important direction for future work.

Similarly, for the 1D model our evaluation was restricted to the
odd-parity eigenstates. As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:simulator-model},
the $n=0$ ground state is not amenable to a finite-grid representation at
all---its wave function is the singular limit $|\psi|^2=\delta(x)$ with
divergent binding energy. The $n>0$ even-parity states are, per Loudon's
analysis, degenerate eigenstates sharing the same
Balmer energies as their odd-parity counterparts
\cite{Loudon1959.10.1119/1.1934950}; however, their first derivative is
discontinuous at the origin, giving their momentum-space amplitude a
$1/k^2$ tail instead of the $1/k^3$ tail of the odd-parity states, which we
use here for their better-converging finite-grid representation
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:simulator-model}). In practice, we were unable to obtain
an accurate time evolution for the even-parity states at the qubit counts
(up to 6 qubits per spatial dimension) explored in this work. We attribute
this to the heavier tail, which requires a wider momentum range---and
hence more qubits---to resolve than the odd-parity states need; however,
we have not determined the minimum qubit count required to
represent an even-parity wave function accurately, so it is not yet
established whether the difficulty is a fundamental limitation of the
present methodology or simply an insufficient-resolution artifact that a
larger qubit count would resolve. Settling this question, and extending
the present singularity-treatment and parameter-selection methodology to
the even-parity states---including the singular ground state---if still
needed, is left for future work.

Regarding circuit implementation, the UCRZ-based approach is best
understood as a design well suited to small-scale, qubit-constrained
systems: within this study it clearly outperformed arithmetic circuits in qubit
count for the 1D and 2D models, but that advantage is lost once the
dimensionality increases to 3D
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:circuit-size-evaluation-results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:hamiltonian-gatecount}),
because the number of controlled rotations scales exponentially with the
size of the coordinate register. Further optimization of the UCRZ
decomposition itself is unlikely to resolve this scaling behavior.
Consistent with prior large-scale first-quantization proposals, scaling
beyond the regime studied here is understood to require a
qualitatively different toolkit: e.g., a QROM-based potential evaluation
combined with a Newton--Raphson-type iterative inversion of the Coulomb
kernel, together with time evolution via quantum signal processing (QSP)
rather than Trotterization. This approach trades a substantially larger
qubit count for asymptotically lower gate (T-)cost and circuit depth than
the arithmetic- or UCRZ-based Trotter approach considered here
\cite{YuanSu.PRXQuantum.2.040332, eklund2026.arXiv2603.19007}. The present
work should therefore be read as establishing an accurate, resource-light
baseline and parameter-selection methodology for this small-scale regime,
rather than as a stepping stone toward scaling the UCRZ circuit itself.

It should be emphasized, however, that this scaling limitation is specific
to the circuit implementation, and not to the two methodological
contributions of this work. The treatment of the Coulomb singularity via
a local average potential and the procedure for selecting the spatial and
temporal discretization parameters both address properties of the
grid-based discretization of the Hamiltonian itself, independently of how
the potential is subsequently evaluated on a circuit. They are therefore
equally applicable to the large-scale, QROM- and QSP-based
first-quantization frameworks discussed above, in which the same
singularity must be regularized and the same grid spacing and time step
must be chosen. In this sense, these findings are expected to provide
practical guidelines for designing and executing grid-based quantum
simulations across scales---from the resource-constrained regime
demonstrated here to fully fault-tolerant implementations.
