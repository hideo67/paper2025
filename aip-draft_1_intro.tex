\section{Introduction}
Since the conception of quantum computers, the simulation of quantum many-body
systems---and quantum chemistry in particular---has been recognized as a highly
promising application area expected to be significantly advanced by quantum
computing \cite{Cao2019.doi:10.1021/acs.chemrev.8b00803}.
Currently, mainstream quantum hardware is inherently prone to computational
errors because of noise, and the practical implementation of error-correction
technologies is ongoing. The surface code method, a promising error-correction
technique for quantum computers, requires a significantly higher level of
redundancy compared with that required by classical error-correction methods
\cite{Fowler2012.PhysRevA.86.032324}. Consequently, realizing a practical
fault-tolerant quantum computer (FTQC) faces implementation challenges,
primarily because of the need to vastly increase the number of qubits on
hardware devices, and is expected to take several more years for development. In
the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, where error correction
remains to be implemented and qubit counts are limited, Variational Quantum
Eigensolver (VQE)-based algorithms utilizing second quantization are the
mainstream of quantum chemistry research \cite{peruzzo2014variational}. VQE
combines a quantum computer with a classical computer to reduce the required
number of qubits and circuit depth, facilitating the execution on NISQ devices
as it can tolerate the presence of noise. However, once FTQC is realized---
which entails overcoming noise  and relaxing qubit count constraints---a broader
range of algorithms is expected to be researched and put into practical use.

The grid-based approach, a representative method for quantum chemistry
simulation based on first quantization, is one of the ultimate targets in this
field as it can directly handle electron interactions as well as electron
structure dynamics because nuclei-centered orbital basis functions are not used.
However, it requires substantially more computational resources than those
required by VQE \cite{kassal2008.pnas.0808245105}. In the grid method, the
indices of equally spaced grid points are mapped to the tensor product of the
computational basis states of multi-qubit registers, each corresponding to
spatial coordinates. The values of the wavefunction at these grid points are
then stored as amplitudes of the corresponding basis to simulate its time
evolution. While algorithms based on the Suzuki-Trotter decomposition are widely
known for time evolution calculations, algorithms employing more sophisticated
approaches have also been proposed \cite{Berry2015.PhysRevLett.114.090502,
Babbush_2018, YuanSu.PRXQuantum.2.040332, eklund2026.arXiv2603.19007}.
Nevertheless, all these grid-based algorithms generally require a
large number of qubits and significant circuit depth, and cannot tolerate
computational errors; therefore reports of their operational verification are strictly
limited. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no calculations using actual
quantum computer hardware have been reported to date; existing reports have
mainly relied on quantum computer emulators running on classical machines. For example,
Benenti et al. performed calculations using a simplified potential function
\cite{Benenti2008.10.1119/1.2894532}. Chan et al. utilized both a quantum
computer emulator and a classical program, processing the Coulomb potential
calculations on the classical side \cite{Chan2023.sciadv.abo7484}. In other
words, an example of computing the Coulomb potential entirely within a quantum
circuit remains to be reported, even on an emulator. Therefore, in this
study, we present the configuration and operational demonstration of a
grid-based simulator implemented entirely using quantum circuits, including the
Coulomb potential calculation. The software used to generate the simulator
circuits is crsQ, which has been developed by the authors
\cite{takahashi2024.doi:10.1021/acs.jctc.4c00708}. We implement the wave
function time evolution calculation via the grid method entirely as a quantum
circuit, execute it on a quantum computer emulator, and evaluate the
computational results.

We emphasize at the outset that the system studied here is a single
electron in one- and two-dimensional hydrogen atom models with a fixed
nucleus. This choice is deliberate rather than incidental: because these
models admit exact analytical solutions, they allow the accuracy of the
singularity treatment and of the discretization parameters to be assessed
against a known reference, without the confounding effects of electron
correlation. The present work is accordingly a proof of principle for the
circuit construction, and---more durably---for a discretization
methodology that is not tied to the particular circuit used here.
Multi-electron simulations and execution on physical hardware are outside
its scope. Within this scope, we address the following three challenges.

The first challenge is to present a circuit configuration that can operate on an
emulator with a limited number of qubits. In this study, we simplify the
computational model to fit within the constraints of the emulator, targeting 1D and
2D models of a single hydrogen atom with fixed nuclear coordinates. The
fundamental approach for calculating the Coulomb potential is based on binary
arithmetic operations \cite{Zalka-rspa.1998.0162}. Although methods have been
proposed to reduce the gate count by precomputing parts of the calculation on a
classical computer and using a QROM-based lookup table, these approaches require
a significant number of ancilla qubits \cite{Sanders2020.PRXQuantum.1.020312}.
In this study, to conserve qubit resources, we adopt the Uniformly Controlled
Rotation Z (UCRZ) circuit \cite{Mottonen2004.PhysRevLett.93.130502}, which
applies rotations to a target qubit based on the values in a control register.
For time-evolution, we employ the basic Suzuki-Trotter
decomposition \cite{Zalka-rspa.1998.0162}. Through this approach, we provide circuit configuration examples that make
full-circuit verification feasible at emulator scale. In the grid method, the scale of the wavefunction initialization
circuit also poses a challenge, because preparing an arbitrary state requires a
number of gates that scale exponentially with the grid size. As a means to
reduce this scale, a method has been proposed to convert the initial states
expressed as linear combinations of atomic orbitals into a Matrix Product State
(MPS) format---or Tensor-Train format
\cite{Oseledets2011.doi:10.1137/090752286}---and construct the initialization
circuit based on low-rank approximations \cite{Huggins2025.PRXQuantum.6.020319}.
This technique can significantly reduce the circuit scale by utilizing a bond
rank corresponding to the smoothness of the wavefunction and the strength of
inter-particle correlations. However, as this study focuses on small-scale
grid sizes feasible for emulation, we utilize simple initialization circuits
without MPS compression to prioritize the ease of implementation. 

The second challenge is to determine how to handle the singularity (pole) of the
Coulomb potential. In the grid method, it is necessary to calculate the
potential energy for all possible combinations of coordinates for all particles
and update the phase of the wavefunction, which is proportional to the energy.
The point where the coordinates of the nucleus and electron coincide is a
singularity of the Coulomb potential, where the value diverges if left
unaddressed. Methods using a plane-wave basis (momentum-space representation)
are known to avoid direct calculations of the singularity in real space by
treating the potential as an operation in momentum space \cite{Babbush_2019,
eklund2026.arXiv2603.19007}. However, these approaches require a significant
number of ancilla qubits, making them unsuitable for execution on small-scale
quantum computers. In contrast, although the position-basis approach can be
implemented with fewer ancilla qubits, it necessitates an explicit strategy for
addressing the singularity. To this end, several techniques have been proposed,
such as employing a soft-core potential that adds a fixed offset to the
inter-particle distance \cite{Kivlichan_2017}, or introducing a relative shift
between the grids for the nucleus and electrons to prevent the distance from
becoming zero \cite{Chan2023.sciadv.abo7484}. In this study, we propose a method
to determine an effective potential value based on the integral average (or
volume average) of the potential function within the grid cell containing the
singularity, and verify its validity through numerical evaluation.

The third challenge is to establish a method for determining simulation
parameters that minimize error within a given number of qubits. In the grid
method, simulation parameters such as the spatial resolution (grid spacing) and
the size of the simulation domain need to be defined first. Furthermore, when
using the Suzuki-Trotter decomposition, the time step size must also be
determined. The Suzuki-Trotter decomposition approximates the exponential of a
Hamiltonian---which contains inherently non-commuting potential and kinetic
energy terms---as a product of individual exponentials. This decomposition
introduces errors arising from the non-commutativity of the operators. Early
error analyses were based on operator norms, evaluating the worst-case scenario
across all possible input states. As a result, these estimates were noted to be
several orders of magnitude larger than the errors empirically observed in
practical numerical calculations. However, by focusing on commutator scaling,
more tight error bounds have become possible
\cite{Childs2021.PhysRevX.11.011020}. Subsequently, a study showed that the
bound could be further tightened by considering state-dependency rather than
relying on worst-case evaluations \cite{Burgarth2024.PhysRevResearch.6.043155}.
The study demonstrated that when the wavefunction value at the potential
singularity is non-zero (e.g., for $s$-orbitals), the error exhibits a sublinear
scaling of $n^{-1/4}$ with respect to the number of Trotter steps $n$. It also
showed that higher-order Trotter formulas do not contribute to error reduction
for such states. This result was extended to multi-particle systems, proving
that for unbounded Hamiltonians like the Coulomb potential, the dependence on
the number of steps $n$ is $n^{-1/4}$, and the error increase relative to the
particle number is polynomial \cite{fang2026trotter}. In our study, using
numerical evaluations based on an emulator and classical programs, we present an
integrated approach for a single-electron model: we optimize spatial parameters
by balancing the tradeoff between the spatial discretization error and domain
truncation error, and determine time parameters by considering the requirements
of the sampling theorem alongside recent Trotter error evaluations.
